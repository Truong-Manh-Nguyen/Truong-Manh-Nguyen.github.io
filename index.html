Research — Master's thesis

Credit-risk modelling on peer-to-peer lending data: benchmarks, feature reliance and evaluation design

University of Pisa · in progress

  • In progress
  • Exploratory results

Question

How do tuned gradient-boosting models and, in a planned next stage, graph neural networks perform on peer-to-peer loan data, and which information do these models actually rely on?

Data and methods

The thesis uses resolved-loan cohorts from LendingClub (United States, 1.34 million loans) and Bondora (Estonia, Finland, Spain and Slovakia, 120,545 loans). As documented in the draft, a feature blacklist is intended to exclude post-origination inputs (origination-time availability remains unverified for three retained Bondora platform outputs); the pipeline prepares encoded and native-categorical representations, trains cost-weighted XGBoost and LightGBM models with staged tuning, and reports average precision (AP) together with the positive rate.

Terms. The positive rate, or prevalence, is the share of loans labelled as defaults and provides a reference for interpreting average precision (AP). AP combines precision at different score thresholds, weighting each by the increase in recall. Grouped permutation importance jointly shuffles selected input columns across loans and measures the resulting change in a fixed model's AP.

Status

The gradient-boosting benchmarks and a feature-reliance audit are complete. Graph models (GraphSAGE) and a comparison of graph explainers (GNNExplainer, PGExplainer) are planned and have not yet been run.

Exploratory results

Verification note. These summaries are based on supplied saved-output and code extracts. Numerical consistency was checked, but the original model runs and predictive validity were not independently verified.

Benchmark. In a historical exploratory benchmark on a random loan-level split of LendingClub resolved loans, tuned LightGBM reached Test AP 0.430 against a positive rate of 0.199 (tuned XGBoost 0.427). This is not an unbiased estimate of performance on unseen loans: preprocessing was fitted on the full cohort including Test, one cleaning rule was target-conditioned, and the LightGBM study was designed after the XGBoost Test results were known.

Reliance on platform scores. In a post-hoc diagnostic of the fixed thesis models, globally permuting a group of platform-assigned grades, pricing variables and platform model outputs reduced AP by an amount equivalent to about 21–24% of AP above prevalence for LendingClub and 24–26% for Bondora. LendingClub used a stratified 20,000-loan evaluation subsample; Bondora used the full Test split. These historical diagnostics depend on the permutation scheme and inherit the thesis's evaluation limitations. They do not establish causation, information substitutability or generalisation performance. The loan-level intervals do not account for borrower dependence and may understate uncertainty, and the origination-time availability of three Bondora outputs remains unverified.

Composition effects. The audit compared model ranking performance with Development-derived country and country-by-issue-year lookup baselines, to investigate composition effects and motivate within-country and temporal evaluation.

Next step

These observations motivate the PhD question of when alternative information can substitute for bureau and platform information in automated credit screening, and how refusals based on it should be explained.

Case study 1 — Decision support for a music-streaming business

Team project (two members, with Thi Hien Do) · Laboratory of Data Science / Decision Support Systems · MSc Data Science and Business Informatics, University of Pisa · 2025/2026 · Prof. Anna Monreale

  • Team course project

Problem

The course brief asked teams to simulate a decision-support system for a music-streaming company, starting from two raw files (track metadata with lyrics, and artist profiles) and progressing from data preparation to a data warehouse, ETL, an OLAP cube and dashboards.

Decision context

In the simulated case the decision-makers are a streaming platform and a record label. The analyses were designed to inform release timing, promotion and content strategy; benefits were proposed, not measured.

What the team built

  • Data preparation. Enriched incomplete artist records using public sources, geocoding (OpenStreetMap Nominatim) and regular expressions, and profiled tracks with K-means on eight acoustic features and topic modelling on lyrics.
  • Warehouse. A snowflake-style schema with a song-level fact table (streams one month after release), a bridge table for the many-to-many song–artist relation and a junk category dimension, loaded by a batched, dependency-ordered Python loader.
  • ETL. SSIS data flows for the course-set queries (yearly artist rankings, summer–winter release ratios, regional stream shares, and a trending/flopping classification with documented edge cases) and two team-proposed queries: a release-timing calendar of trending outcomes by month and weekday, and the association between explicit content and trending/flopping outcomes by lyric category.
  • OLAP and BI. An SSAS cube with time and geography hierarchies; MDX queries for role-weighted (0.8/0.2) stream allocation, year-over-year and seasonal benchmarks, and a team-proposed top-artists-by-segment query; Power BI dashboards.

Why it matters for my research

The project is a compact example of the information-systems view I want to bring to automated decision-making: the analytical value depends on how decision-relevant information is modelled, integrated and delivered to a decision-maker, not only on the analytical method.

Limitations

This is coursework on a simulated case: no organisational benefit was measured, and the analyses describe associations within a course dataset.

Case study 2 — Research internship: small-object detection for anti-littering monitoring

2025 International Internship Pilot Program in Taiwan · Research Center on AI and Sustainable Development · supervised by Prof. Pao-Ann Hsiung (Dept. of Computer Science and Information Engineering, National Chung Cheng University)

  • Research internship
  • Report-stated figures, not verified

Problem

Detecting litter and discarded cigarettes in public-space video is hard: the report describes the objects as extremely small, often blurry, and, for cigarettes, fast-moving at the moment of discarding.

Approach

I fine-tuned RF-DETR, a DETR-family real-time detector, for two tasks: littering and cigarette detection. For littering, the training data combined project CCTV footage with the public TACO dataset.

Reported results — figures as stated in the internship report

Littering: test mAP@50 0.675, and 88.9% of littering events detected across 36 test videos. Cigarettes: best validation mAP@50 0.40, and 77.8% of events detected across 9 test videos.

Note. These figures are not independently verified. The report does not define a "successful detection"; a training-curve figure in the report shows cigarette AP50 above the stated 0.40, and a saved notebook evaluation on the littering test images reports mAP@50 0.75 rather than 0.675. Evaluation footage is confidential.

Limitations

Small evaluation samples; a domain gap between static TACO trash and moving discards; extremely small or occluded objects.
